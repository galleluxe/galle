## India's Ancient Perfume Legacy

The word *attar* comes from the Arabic *itr* — meaning perfume — but its roots are Sanskrit: *atra*, meaning smell. India has been creating complex aromatic compositions for over five millennia. The methods survive in Kannauj, a small city in Uttar Pradesh sometimes called the "Grasse of the East."

### Deg-Bhapka: Hydro-Distillation

Kannauj's artisans use a technique called **deg-bhapka**, a form of hydro-distillation performed entirely without electricity. A copper still (*deg*) filled with botanical material is heated over a wood fire. The steam passes through a bamboo pipe into a copper receiver (*bhapka*) submerged in cold water. The condensed liquid — a mixture of water and volatile aromatics — collects in sandalwood oil, which absorbs the scent molecules while the water evaporates through the porous walls of the earthen vessel.

The result is an attar: a concentrated, oil-based perfume of extraordinary richness and complexity.

### The Materials of Indian Perfumery

India's aromatic heritage draws on an extraordinary botanical portfolio:

- **Oud (Agarwood)**: Resinous heartwood from *Aquilaria* trees infected by a specific mould. The scent is smoky, animalic, complex — and the basis of our **White Oud** fragrance.
- **Vetiver (Khus)**: Roots of *Chrysopogon zizanioides*, grown in the Deccan plateau. Earthy, smoky, with a surprising freshness.
- **Tuberose (Rajnigandha)**: Intensely narcotic and creamy; used in **Nuit Blanche**.
- **Jasmine Sambac**: India's native jasmine variety — rounder and more indolic than its Grasse counterpart.
